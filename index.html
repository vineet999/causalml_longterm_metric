You might have observed that every metric you chose as your winner was solving something useful. But yet it seemed more short-term than anything else. Why so? Let's take a step back-

  1. Who are the people here? - Trekkers & businesses (We exclude monastery since it is an independent cultural landmark and not a commercial entity)
  2. Primary motivation? - Trekkers are seeking a rejuvenating experience- overcoming jitters to begin the climb, pushing through stretches to achieve summit euphoria, and anchoring the sacred memory at the monastery, cafe and souvenir shop. Local businesses are seeking to serve these customers through this journey and earn a predictable, sustainable revenue.

Whatever value we bring must cater to both users to sustain the ecosystem.

  1. Prima facie, since this trail is remote, improving acquisition is intricate and may not be enough to keep the small businesses alive.
  2. Conversely, Summiters Expense Rate (%Summiters who purchase at cafe or souvenir shop) might feel more appropriate; It indeed measures immediate value capture, but it fundamentally relies on a steady foot traffic.

So, for Pahadi, driving trekker retention is what creates a sustainable visitor and business growth at the same time.

With that established, we now move to the next question- How do you evaluate retention (a delayed metric) through a short experiment? You cannot run the experiment forever to observe actual retention due to high execution costs. And short signals in isolation may or may not directionally align with your long-term metric.

The solution is surrogate metrics: measurable short-term behaviors that predict long-term success.

Often, in online product experimentation, isolated short-term metrics rarely capture the effect of the long-term metric. Leveraging surrogates bridges the gap between immediate experiment results and lasting retention.

How it works, in short:

Imagine you are working at YouTube testing a new feature designed to increase consumer retention. Consumer here is the user who streams and consumes content. For this case, let's assume it's a two-week experiment.

You first measure the short-term signals over the course of two weeks: this can be watch hours, active days, subscriptions etc.

But you still want to know how these signals help in evaluating the long-term goal.

So you look at your archive of old experiments, far enough back to have the actual retention data. An experiment evaluating 12-month retention must have spent at least a year to qualify here. Now, you have both: the two-week signals back then, and what actually happened later. Learn the pattern between them once, and you have built a translator — early signals in, long-term retention out.

Run your new test for two weeks and it tells you the likely retention impact.

You get a decision now instead of three, six or twelve months from now.
